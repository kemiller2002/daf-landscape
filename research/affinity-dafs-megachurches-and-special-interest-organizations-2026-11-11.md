# DAFs in Faith, Megachurch and Affinity Networks: Business Models, Opportunity and Clarity Constraints
**Research cutoff 2026-11-11. First-stage public-source and structural analysis, not a completed buyer/contract census.**

## Executive understanding

Faith and affinity communities are **a major under-covered market segment**, not simply DAF grant recipients. They can serve as: (1) a community or affinity sponsor of donor-advised accounts, (2) a religious/nonprofit **recipient** of grants from donor funds, (3) an organizer of donor relationships and family legacy plans, (4) a nonprofit that establishes an **organization-advised or designated fund** with a different public charity, or (5) a distribution network for outside sponsors and investment advisers.

**Hypothesis to test:** institutions with durable, high-trust donor relationships may benefit from outsourced *complex-asset acceptance, sponsor compliance, charitable accounting, donor succession, reporting, grant settlement and fund portability*. This does **not** establish an addressable software market or justify shifting normal gifts to a DAF.

**Major negative result:** Ordinary tax-qualified direct cash donations to a church or other eligible charity often need no DAF. For non-itemizers in **2026**, qualifying direct cash contributions may be eligible for up to **$1,000 single / $2,000 joint** additional deduction, but gifts *to DAFs* are excluded [AF15]. Claiming a DAF is a universally advantageous substitute for routine giving would fail Clarity.

### Empirical market context

- The Hartford Institute's July 2026 published study estimates **approximately 1,850 US Protestant megachurches**, using a >=2,000 weekend attendance criterion, and surveyed **589** congregations with 900+ attendees, including **331** megachurches [AF01,AF28]. It reports 67% of the megachurch sample exceeding pre-pandemic attendance, with community programs and giving broadly rebounding. **Attendance is not donors, investible DAF accounts or paid technology contracts.** The institute's separate megachurch directory **explicitly forbids its commercial reuse**; we did not copy or scrape it [AF02].
- [National Christian Foundation](https://www.ncfgiving.com/giving-fund) lists **$468 million in 2025 grants to churches**, plus support for missions, humanitarian assistance and other categories [AF03]. This category includes unknown-size churches, not just megachurches, and does not show software vendor fee revenue.
- [The Signatry](https://thesignatry.com/2024-report/) reported **more than $746 million in overall 2024 grants** and describes nonliquid gift and charitable investment services [AF04]. **Do not compare this all-cause 2024 figure directly with NCF's 2025 church category.**
- [Jewish Communal Fund](https://jcfny.org/2026impactreport/) reports **$1.3 billion in 2026 fundholder giving** [AF07] and publishes audit/990 information [AF08]. This is sponsor-specific fundholder activity, not software revenue, nor all Jewish donor giving.
- [American Muslim Community Foundation](https://amuslimcf.org/dafs/) reports a DAF program serving **over 200 families**, faith-compatible investments and zakat-focused advising [AF11,AF25]. Its own older and current product pages **show different fee tables**, requiring a signed fee schedule before any total-cost comparison [AF11,AF12].
- [Catholic foundations](https://www.thecatholicfoundation.org/giving/donor-advised-funds/) offer DAFs alongside designated parish and school endowments and succession plans [AF09,AF10]. Those different products must not be combined as one DAF count.
- [WaterStone](https://waterstone.org/charity-advised-fund/) explicitly offers a *Charity Advised Fund* designed for churches and nonprofits, supporting appreciated assets and organization grant recommendations [AF06,AF27]. **The adjective "advised" does not, on its own, settle the fund's legal DAF classification.**

## 1. How this works in practice: distinct transactions

### Model A: Direct contribution to a church or affinity charity

`Donor → eligible church/charity → actual charitable program`.

For cash giving that fulfills a donor's present intent, especially a non-itemizer eligible for the 2026 direct cash deduction, this is often the simplest pathway. The organization retains the charitable asset and bears any processor costs. **No DAF account or separate sponsoring organization is necessary.**

### Model B: Donor's independent faith-aligned DAF makes grants to church

`Donor irrevocably contributes assets → NCF / The Signatry / other eligible public DAF sponsor → sponsor-approved grant → qualified church or ministry`.

The sponsor, not the donor and not necessarily the church, controls the charitable assets. Donor advisers can recommend recipients/investments subject to the sponsor's rules [AF16,AF21]. This is more relevant for appreciated assets, high-income giving years, multi-beneficiary charitable plans, family succession and asset gifts requiring professional acceptance. A nonbinding donor preference is not a contracted promise that the church will get the cash. Some donors will prefer a mainstream financial-institution DAF and grant to a religious charity without selecting an affinity sponsor.

### Model C: Church or charity establishes an organization-advised/designated fund

`Donors/organization → externally sponsored charitable fund → organization-advised grants or designated purpose`.

WaterStone markets this model to churches and charities [AF06]. Legal fund types vary. An agency endowment benefiting one parish, a discretionary church-advised fund, and an individual donor-advised fund can have different legal control, beneficiary rights, tax treatment and reporting. The **signed agreement** is the unit of analysis.

### Model D: An affinity foundation actually sponsors donors' DAFs

`Charity sponsor (identified tax-exempt legal entity) maintains separate advised funds and governance → eligible recipients`.

JCF, AMCF, NCF, The Signatry and Catholic foundations illustrate variations [AF03,AF04,AF07,AF09,AF11]. Products may add values-aligned investment choices, charitable eligibility/mission guidelines, local relationship advisers and shared grantmaking. These differences may create real donor utility, but fee, recipient-choice restrictions and actual philanthropic benefit must be made transparent.

### Model E: A cause-specific nonprofit accepts grants without sponsoring DAFs

`Independent DAF sponsor → approved grant → issue-related charity / hospital / university / disaster organization`.

An issue-focused public charity may promote DAF giving and recommend outside sponsor options. [Alliance Defending Freedom's DAF giving instructions](https://www.adfplannedgiving.org/legacy-giving/donor-advised-fund.html) recommend NCF and The Signatry [AF24], but this does **not** mean the recipient operates either fund or that political campaign activity is an eligible DAF expenditure. A distinct nonprofit's educational/public charity status must be checked independently. A donor must be free to give directly or decline.

## 2. Where the technology and commercial opportunity might be

| Use case | Potential institutional buyer | Real user job and proposed technology | What proves actual value |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Advanced charitable asset acceptance** | Church network, church foundation, community sponsor | Facilitate stock, business interests, real estate, proposed valuation, sponsor acceptance and receipts | Additional accepted eligible gifts *net of costs*, exact donor legal/tax treatment and recipient cash receipt |
| **Outsourced affinity fund administration** | Church/denomination, faith foundation, independent sponsor | Accounts, investment units, grant approval, fund rights, restrictions, fee accounting | Signed institution contract and lower correct completed cost than existing sponsor or direct fund |
| **Succession and legacy planning** | Charitable foundation, major-gift donor advisory unit | Donor-selected successor, beneficiary choices, export, endowment strategy, grant history | Actual funded successor designations and rightful grants across years |
| **Organization-advised fund treasury** | Church, parish network, university, social-service coalition | Fund accounting, custody, investment restrictions and approved grants | Audited balances, permissions and actual improved program deployment net of annual fee |
| **Faith-compatible compliance configuration** | Eligible sponsor with voluntary faith-oriented products | Configurable investment screens, approved grant purpose/timing and independent community expertise | Qualified legal/faith review and outcomes without excluding eligible recipients by automated guesswork |
| **Cross-border humanitarian grants** | Mission network, national affinity sponsor, relief charity | Grant eligibility, foreign recipient documentation, approved payments, FX and actual settlement | Verified sponsor grants to beneficiaries with full legal and financial reconciliation |
| **Consent-aware donor acknowledgement** | Church foundation and nonprofit recipient networks | Respect donor anonymity, lawful ID matching and grant receipt/thank-you automation | Higher *accurate opted-in* attribution, fewer mismatched records, measurable stewardship cost |
| **Transparent comparative giver guidance** | Sponsor and wealth advisory partners | Choose direct gift, DAF, organization endowment, pooled fund or **no gift**, with fees and law | Better reviewed decisions across held-out cases, including DAF rejection when inappropriate |
| **Charitable impact and program results** | Affinity sponsor, large charities, alliances | Grant destinations + actual beneficiary service and program data | Independently verified outcomes rather than grant volume alone |
| **Incident and institution succession** | National sponsor, network foundation | Complete portability on church split, product acquisition or insolvency | Legal sponsor and fund records remain usable and all rights are preserved |

**Potential operating/business model (not proven):** sell B2B software/advisory integration and audit tools to **legal nonprofit sponsors and large organizations**, not through undisclosed individual donor profiling or religious persuasion. Institutions can choose vendor-only licensed software, managed administration or outside sponsorship based on their legal authority. Charge for completed operational value and disclosed contracted services, not from invented "total faith giving × bps" estimates.

### Why fee transparency matters

WaterStone's public Charity Advised Fund page advertises an **annual 1% fee on the first $1m**, tiers thereafter, an optional advisor-managed investment threshold and a **3% one-time charge for some nontraditional asset contributions** [AF06,AF27]. The Signatry describes a substantially different nonliquid-gift stakeholder grant schedule and grants without ordinary payment charges [AF05]. AMCF's two public pages disclose conflicting DAF administration rates [AF11,AF12].

These are **different products, different units and possibly different publication vintages**. We cannot conclude one sponsor is cheaper overall without a standardized gift, account size, asset type, grant calendar, date and contract. The right DAF technology comparison should model **donor benefit, nonprofit net amount, sponsor admin, advisor compensation, investments and acceptance costs** separately.

## 3. A high-value structural surprise: religious charities do not yield a complete Form 990 dataset

Under IRS rules, churches and certain religious organizations that qualify for exemption may be eligible recipients **without requesting formal recognition**, and churches/integrated auxiliaries are generally **not required to file the annual Form 990** [AF14,AF29]. A branded Catholic, Jewish, Muslim or evangelical **public charity foundation** might still be a separate entity with different filing duties. The 990 exemption is based on **legal classification**, not religious name or size.

**Implication for our market analysis:** IRS recipient Form 990 panels will undercount some religious institution financial flows and cannot reveal all DAF grants to churches. This is a data missingness problem, **not evidence of wrongdoing** or proof churches have fewer financial controls. We should use:

- DAF **sponsor-side** grant recipient data where disclosed, by recipient legal name/EIN and purpose.
- Voluntary audited financials and annual reports from individual networks and affiliated foundations.
- Sponsor-by-sponsor grant categories with no fabricated attribution of all faith donations to DAFs.
- Consent-safe nonprofit receipt data and actual member-level donor permission where permitted.
- A documented selection model for organizations absent from the public IRS filing frame.

**Do not reuse Hartford's database as a commercial lead list.** Its own terms explicitly forbid commercial reproduction [AF02]; the 2026 published aggregate study can inform research and sampling at the aggregate level.

## 4. Critical boundaries: special-interest groups are not one tax category

Faith, professional associations, veterans' groups, alumni communities, issue advocates, unions, labor groups, chambers and political committees operate under different federal exemptions and donation rules [AF18,AF26].

- **Church or other qualifying religious 501(c)(3):** a DAF sponsor may approve eligible charitable grants, subject to legal sponsor discretion and no private benefit [AF14,AF16,AF21].
- **Issue-oriented 501(c)(3):** may carry on permitted education and some nonpartisan policy work, but **cannot intervene in candidate election campaigns** [AF17].
- **501(c)(4) social welfare and 501(c)(6) trade association:** contributions generally don't qualify as deductible *charitable* donations. A DAF sponsor may be able to make a tightly restricted **exclusively charitable** grant to certain non-501(c)(3) entities subject to expenditure responsibility and applicable sponsor policy, but this is a legal exception requiring detailed review, **not a general-purpose DAF funding channel for lobbying, trade dues or politics** [AF18,AF26].
- **Candidate, PAC, party, 527 election activity:** DAF-held 501(c)(3) funds **cannot be used as a campaign finance channel**. The candidate campaign prohibition applies to charities and their managers [AF17].
- **Member/student/family benefits:** DAFs cannot simply pay tuition, event tickets, member dues or personal benefits for the donor/adviser. IRS section 4967 and 2017 proposed interpretations make donor-benefit treatment a core control [AF19–AF21].
- **Church tithes/pledges:** a pure sponsor-approved grant may be possible if no donor benefit and no legally disallowed obligation; **pledges and sponsor policies require case review**, so do not blanket promise the ability to satisfy binding donor obligations [AF19].
- **Religious giving rules:** claim of "zakat-compliant" or values-aligned investing is not a substitute for qualified faith interpretation and donor-specific preferences [AF11,AF13].

**The product must allow a truthful "this is not DAF-eligible or not a good DAF use" conclusion.**

## 5. Clarity: ten meaningful hypotheses to test

1. **Megachurch size proves B2B software demand.** Not justified: attendance and community giving cannot become paid contracts without procurement evidence [AF01].
2. **NCF's $468m to churches equals megachurch DAF market size.** Not justified: the category has no megachurch split or total market population [AF03].
3. **Every routine donor should open a DAF.** Contradicted by 2026 nonitemizer rules and possible extra fees; direct giving frequently better [AF15].
4. **Faith alignment causes larger charity impact.** Untested: association and shared mission don't establish causal additionality or lower costs [AF03,AF04].
5. **Organization-advised fund means a tax-defined DAF.** Not established without donor-advisory and sponsor-control facts [AF06,AF16].
6. **Religious 990 nonfilers are noncharitable/opaque by choice.** Incorrect: statutory exemptions; look for voluntary reports and legal entity exceptions [AF14].
7. **All faith sponsors have the same investments and recipient rules.** Untrue or at least undocumented; compare actual sponsor agreements and donor choices [AF03,AF11].
8. **A sponsor's public annual fees establish actual total cost.** Not justified with overlapping donor, advisor, processing, investment and special asset costs; AMCF's competing fee pages illustrate version risk [AF05,AF06,AF11,AF12].
9. **Cause/mission advocacy permits partisan candidate donations through a DAF.** No: legal boundaries around section 501(c)(3) prohibit candidate campaigns [AF17,AF26].
10. **Affinity network branding is a defensible software moat.** Working commercial thesis only until signed sponsor contracts, member conversion and willingness-to-pay are observed.

The detailed [18-question affinity Clarity audit](../data/affinity-daf-clarity-audit-2026.csv) sets observation, assumptions, alternatives, current justified understanding, posture and falsification data for all these and additional topics.

## 6. Proposed proof-oriented sector research sequence

**P0: Build the legal and fund model census.** Identify 501(c)(3) sponsors, organization-advised funds, church recipients, denominational foundations, donor giving circles, and affiliate fees. Map actual **role**, not brand. Sample NCF, The Signatry, WaterStone, JCF, AMCF, a Catholic diocesan foundation and general financial-institution sponsor.

**P0: Model donor choice by **genuine philanthropic need**, not institution incentives.** Test 2026 donors who give regular small church cash gifts, high-income households with appreciated securities, business-exit gifts, charitable bequests, donor anonymity, and urgent ministry grants. Have independent tax review for legal conclusions and faith-domain review only when the institution offers explicit faith conditions.

**P0: Trace actual grant receipts and costs.** A charity can have a large donor base yet prefer direct cash. Measure per transaction, per complex gift, per funded account, per approved external grant, and recipient settlement (avoid treating grant recommendations as money received). Survey institutions about real budgets and existing alternative platforms.

**P1: Validate underserved network workflows.** Church networks, diocesan schools, emergency relief, zakat programs, Jewish federations and community groups may need differentiated timing/beneficiary restrictions, but hypotheses require consented institutional examples and independent outcomes.

**P1: Price and compare real contracts.** Public pages allow provisional *fee hypotheses*; validate actual current rate, waivers, asset acceptance, outside investment charges and who pays. Standardize hypothetical account and giving horizons and include 2026 direct-gift alternatives.

**P1: Reuse F# Clarity/EDF decision engine.** Add sourced claim objects for direct vs DAF vs organization fund; only apply EDF to **observed** failures (delayed grants, donor loss of historical records, erroneous eligibility). Clarity handles unsupported sector-wide business conclusions and reversibility.

**Current best conclusion:** There appears to be a **real institution-level need for advanced charitable financial infrastructure in affinity networks**, exemplified by current faith DAF and charity-advised products. But the strongest plausible first product isn't "DAF for every churchgoer"; it is **direct-gift-compatible sponsor and organization infrastructure** that supports complex assets, independent fund accounting, appropriate advice, and lawful settlement when an actual DAF/organization fund is beneficial.
