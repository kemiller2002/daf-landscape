## Answer

**Only two adjacent industries justify further desk investigation now, and neither justifies building anything before Unbound has a live sponsor.** Both are **charitable gift administration** (gift annuities, charitable remainder trusts, pooled income funds) and **comprehensive fiscal sponsorship**. Each shares Unbound's core ledger pattern: restricted sub-funds under one legal owner, disbursement controls, payee vetting and annual tax reporting. Each also sells to buyers Unbound already targets, or to buyers that are public charities able to sponsor a DAF.

The other industries are either served by entrenched suites (grants and scholarships, trust accounting, private-foundation administration), regulated in ways that share almost nothing with DAFs (escrow, 1031 intermediaries, custodial accounts), or better treated as **buyers of an Unbound component** than as products to build (employee giving buying charity evidence).

Shared Echelon infrastructure does spread **engineering and control cost**: one ledger, one control catalogue, one evidence pipeline, one ingestion engine. It does **not** spread **industry-specific compliance cost**. Gift annuities bring state insurance-style regulation (P3-CLM-FGJ012), trusts bring fiduciary regulation, and private foundations bring a different excise regime. The right form is **separate products on shared components**, not a general-purpose platform. Foundation Source shows the pattern works for an incumbent: it runs private foundations, DAF accounts and PG Calc planned-giving administration under one roof (P3-CLM-FGJ013). That is evidence of a scope economy for a scaled operator, not evidence that an entrant should start broad.

None of these is justified by the "AI-native" framing. The overlap is in **deterministic ledger, controls and evidence**, which Phase II already showed are not proprietary advantages by themselves (CLM-011, Phase II [13](../competitive-displacement/13-compounding-competitive-advantages.md)).

## 3. Does shared Echelon infrastructure spread cost?

| Cost layer | Spreads across adjacent products? | Mechanism | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ledger, controls, sponsor segregation, append-only records | **Yes** | One component used by several product modules (CNV-ARC-001 modular monolith) | Each module adds invariants and test surface; regression risk rises with every module |
| Control catalogue and SOC evidence | **Partly** | echelon-assurance's unified control model is designed so further requirements attach to the same controls (P3-SRC-FGJ001: design-stage, no control above Designed) | Audit scope and cost grow with each in-scope system; SOC 1 reliance differs by product |
| Ingestion, reconciliation, migration (Transitus) | **Yes, if built** | Format and check libraries are domain-neutral by design | Transitus is phase 0 (P3-CLM-FGJ004) |
| UI, documents, hosting boundary (Forma, Folio, Limen) | **Yes** | Already shared libraries | Licence gate (P3-CLM-FGJ002) |
| Industry-specific regulation (CGA reserves and permits; trust fiduciary rules; QI bonding; PF excise rules) | **No** | Each needs its own counsel, filings and possibly capital reserves (held by the issuing charity) | Regulatory exposure multiplies with each industry |
| Sales, implementation, support | **Weakly** | Same buyer (CF) for DAF + planned giving; different buyers elsewhere | Fragmented positioning; a one-founder company (the model assumes one founder FTE) cannot run several sales motions. Revision 2 (P3-CHL-REV1005): previously "a two-person company" |

**Conclusion.** Shared components lower the incremental **engineering** cost of a second charitable module, perhaps substantially for planned giving (unmeasured; P3-HYP-FGJ001). They do not lower **compliance, sales or support** cost, and those dominate at entry (Phase II CLM-040, CLM-043). The evidence supports **separate products on shared components, added only when a live customer asks**. It does not support platform expansion. An adjacent module also falls under DEC-008: a gated investment with its own milestone and termination criteria.

## 4. Challenges to this analysis

- **Selection bias.** Industries were screened for DAF-ledger overlap, which favours look-alikes. An adjacency with different workflows but a stronger buying trigger would rank low here. No such trigger was found in public sources.
- **Foundation Source is the counter-example.** The one firm that spans PF, DAF and planned-giving administration did so partly by **acquisition** (three acquisitions in 2024-25, Phase II CLM-032; how and when PG Calc joined was not verified here), not only by building on shared components. Scope economies may come from customer access more than from shared code.
- **Unknown sizes.** Planned-giving issuer counts and fiscal-sponsor software spend are unknown (P3-GAP-FGJ005, P3-GAP-FGJ006). The ranking is about overlap, not revenue.

## 5. Sequence implication

P3-SEQ-FGJ005: an adjacent planned-giving module is a **36-60 month expansion option** funded by an existing sponsor customer. It is not an entry route. It does not change DEC-001's partner-first entry or DEC-002's deferral of administration.
