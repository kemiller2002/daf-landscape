# Fund managers: DAF cross-business opportunity research track
**2026-11-11 | Future investigation, structured now; not an endorsement of cross-selling**

## Core thesis to investigate
DAFs can be an entry point, retention mechanism or adjacent service channel for investment managers, asset managers, wealth managers, banks and institutional fund administrators. The relevant unit is **incremental, risk-adjusted enterprise value**, not DAF account count or charitable assets alone.

## Opportunity map
| Adjacent business | Mechanism | Potential upside | Critical test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Investment management | Sponsor invests DAF pools in manager funds or mandates | Recurring fee-bearing assets | Is manager actually selected, and are fees competitive and disclosed? |
| Wealth/adviser retention | Adviser maintains relationship through philanthropic planning | Lower attrition, broader planning relationship | Causal retention vs self-selection |
| Complex-asset gifts | Accept appreciated private assets and liquidate appropriately | High-value planning and sponsor differentiation | Appraisal, unrelated business income, liquidity and control risk |
| Institutional outsourced CIO | Manager supports charities, foundations, endowments | Separate institutional mandates | Independent procurement; no presumed grant-recipient conversion |
| Treasury/cash management | Charities and sponsors need cash, settlement and liquidity | Deposits, treasury services, float where legally permitted | Cash ownership, fiduciary restrictions, market rates and conflicts |
| Employer philanthropy | Workplace giving, matching, employee DAFs | Distribution and employer relationships | Employer adoption, acquisition cost, data rights |
| Estate/legacy planning | DAF successor and bequest planning | Multigenerational relationship continuity | Estate-law constraints, client consent and actual conversion |
| Grant recipient services | Nonprofits receiving grants need fundraising, treasury and investment services | Potential new B2B relationship | No donor-data exploitation, inappropriate steering or implied access |
| Technology/administration | White-label DAF services to other institutions | Software/operations revenue | Real product margin, implementation cost, compliance and switching |
| Lending/private banking | Broader client needs alongside charitable planning | Client lifetime value | No assumption that donated assets are personal collateral |

## Distinguish business types
1. Asset managers manufacture investment products; they may not own the client relationship.
2. Wealth managers/advisers distribute advice and may direct eligible DAF investment allocations.
3. Custodians and banks handle custody, deposits, cash and settlement.
4. Legal DAF sponsors own charitable assets and make grant decisions.
5. Administrators and platform vendors provide services without necessarily controlling assets.
6. Operating nonprofits are independent recipients, not captive commercial leads.

## Model
For each opportunity: incremental revenue = eligible reachable base × actual adoption × average realized net fee, **less cannibalization**. Incremental contribution = revenue - servicing - distribution - technology - compliance - risk costs. Discount over retention period; compare with counterfactual where no DAF product is offered. Do not count the same AUM or client fee twice across affiliates.

Example scenarios should vary asset mix, balances, payout timing, fund fees, adoption and retention; illustrative figures must be explicitly marked assumptions, not reported results.

## Clarity challenges
- **Correlation vs causation:** Wealthier clients may both open DAFs and stay with their adviser; DAF use may not cause retention.
- **Revenue vs value:** Capturing an affiliated fund fee may merely shift revenue from another product.
- **AUM illusion:** Assets in DAF accounts are owned by the sponsor and can be granted out; they are not permanent adviser-controlled assets.
- **Regulatory mismatch:** Fiduciary duties, charitable purposes, prohibited benefits, donor-adviser restrictions, conflicts, privacy and sponsor discretion limit commercial exploitation.
- **Recipient fallacy:** Grant recipients are not automatically suitable prospects or permitted marketing leads.
- **Scale fallacy:** Broad service offerings can add complexity faster than they create revenue.

## EDF diagnostic questions
Who controls the relationship? Who has permission to use data? Where is investment discretion located? Which entity bears fiduciary/compliance responsibility? Which handoff creates cost or delay? Can a sponsor switch manager/administrator without loss of records? What outcomes improve for donors and charities, not just affiliated businesses?

## Research requirements
A. Sponsor investment policies and published fees.
B. Parent-company segment disclosures and advisory/custody economics.
C. Asset-management affiliations and fee waivers.
D. Sponsor legal terms and donor-adviser investment discretion.
E. Actual program adoption and donor retention where published.
F. Evidence of separate nonprofit institutional-service mandates.
G. Regulatory opinions and enforcement on conflicts, excess benefits, private benefit and donor control.
H. Alternative explanations and counterfactuals.

## Decision outputs
- Opportunity attractiveness versus evidence strength matrix.
- Economics by legal entity with affiliate eliminations.
- Conflict and reputational risk register.
- Comparative moat: distribution, investment products, charitable trust, data, technology, service and regulatory competence.
- Research-backed conclusions and explicit unresolved claims.
