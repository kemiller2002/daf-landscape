# DAFs as a Client Relationship Continuity Strategy for Wealth Managers
**Date:** 2026-11-11
**Status:** Research hypothesis and study protocol; not an empirically established retention effect.

## Strategic thesis
A donor-advised fund contribution transfers legal ownership of donated assets to a charitable sponsor, but need not terminate the donor's relationship with their financial adviser. The firm may continue to serve the household through planning, sponsor-approved investment mandates, philanthropic strategy, and multigenerational engagement. **Relationship retention, asset retention, and fee retention are different outcomes** and must never be conflated.

## Relationship continuity mechanisms
1. **Investment continuity:** If sponsor permits and approves an external manager, adviser may manage sponsor-owned charitable investments. This is not donor ownership or guaranteed advisory fee retention.
2. **Planning continuity:** Tax-aware appreciated-asset selection before donation, charitable budget planning, estate coordination, liquidity and grantmaking guidance. Tax/legal advice requires appropriate professionals.
3. **Family continuity:** Giving conversations may engage spouses, adult children and successors. Participation does not prove future client conversion or retention.
4. **Institutional continuity:** Adviser may coordinate sponsor, custodian, charity, tax counsel and estate counsel while preserving donor privacy and sponsor authority.
5. **Service continuity:** Shared reporting and grant visibility may reduce fragmentation even where investment assets are no longer managed by the original adviser.

## Central falsifiable hypothesis
Wealth-management firms with integrated, client-centered philanthropic planning may experience higher household retention and multigenerational engagement than comparable firms without it, **after controlling for client wealth, tenure, age, philanthropy propensity, adviser quality, market returns and selection effects**.

### Counterhypotheses
- DAF recommendations can be motivated by retaining fee-bearing investment mandates; perceived conflicts may undermine trust.
- Charitable clients may already be unusually loyal; simple comparisons create selection bias.
- Contributions can reduce personally owned AUM and advisory revenue even if relationship survives.
- Family philanthropy does not necessarily translate into heirs becoming clients.
- Sponsor-owned investments may be restricted to internal pools; relationship continuity may be non-revenue.
- Some clients value philanthropic independence from their financial adviser.

## Outcomes and metrics
| Outcome | Definition | Evidence needed |
|---|---|---|
| Household retention | Continued advisory relationship at 12/24/36 months | Anonymized CRM cohort data |
| Net fee retention | Total recurring household + permitted sponsor-mandate fees, less cannibalized taxable AUM fees | Billing and sponsor mandate data |
| Assets continuity | Sponsor-approved adviser-managed assets retained, separated from client-owned AUM | Sponsor program approvals and custody data |
| Heir engagement | Consented meetings, planning relationships and conversion, not simply number of heirs named | Cohort-level engagement records |
| Client trust | Documented satisfaction, complaints and conflict understanding | Independent interviews and structured surveys |
| Philanthropic outcomes | Grants completed, recipient satisfaction, time-to-resolution, donor goal achievement | Sponsor and recipient process data |

## Study design
**Phase 1: Desk research**. Compare Fidelity Charitable, DAFgiving360, NPT, AEF, Vanguard Charitable, Morgan Stanley GIFT and selected community foundations for sponsor-approved investment management, successor privileges, adviser access, reporting, fees and privacy.
**Phase 2: Qualitative interviews**. Interview RIAs with and without DAF integration, sponsor staff, clients and nonprofit recipients. Ask for concrete cases and counterexamples, not generic endorsements.
**Phase 3: Retention analysis**. Compare matched cohorts (or difference-in-differences around a documented program launch), predeclare outcomes, adjust for confounders and report uncertainty. Avoid causal claims from cross-sectional correlations.
**Phase 4: Economics**. Model net fee effects of charitable contributions, incremental planning fees, sponsor-approved investment mandates, acquisition and servicing costs, and client lifetime value without counting sponsor-owned assets as personal client AUM.
**Phase 5: Governance**. Review fiduciary disclosures, sponsor control, donor consent, privacy, succession rules and incentives for adviser recommendations.

## Pave Finance case study
Pave's potential role is an RIA-side relationship-continuity layer: identify appreciated lots and funding implications **before** contribution; document sponsor eligibility and investment mandates; coordinate reporting and grant liquidity **after** contribution where authorized. Fidelity/Schwab custodian integrations do not automatically authorize sponsor-system access. Test whether advisers will pay for continuity workflows independently of portfolio optimization.

## Clarity and EDF
**Clarity:** distinguish observed relationships, hypotheses, conflicts and causal evidence. Explicitly test 'DAFs improve retention' against the alternative 'loyal clients disproportionately use DAFs.'
**EDF:** map donor→adviser→custodian→sponsor→investment manager→recipient handoffs; diagnose information loss, conflicting ownership, fee incentives, permission gaps, successor changes and accountability for exceptions.

## Decisions and evidence gates
- Do not call DAFs a proven retention strategy until longitudinal or quasi-experimental evidence supports it.
- Do not equate sponsor assets with adviser-managed assets.
- Do not recommend DAF contributions merely to preserve adviser revenue.
- Advance Pave pilot only with sponsor-authorized workflows, demonstrated adviser demand, net economics and transparent conflicts.
- Publish both positive and negative retention findings.
