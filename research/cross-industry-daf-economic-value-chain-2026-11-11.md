# Cross-industry DAF economic value-chain analysis
**Research framework | 2026-11-11 | Status: analytical design, not a completed financial audit**

## Central question
For a donor-advised fund contribution, who controls each decision, who bears costs and risks, who receives direct and indirect economic benefits, and when does money reach an operating charity? Compare business models rather than anchoring on any vendor.

## Unit of analysis
Trace a **$100,000 illustrative contribution** over a specified holding period. This is a modeling convention, not an industry average or a claim about any sponsor. Track donated asset type, sponsor legal ownership, tax deduction timing/eligibility, liquidation or in-kind acceptance, investment allocation, adviser involvement, grants and settlement. Model time, returns and fees explicitly.

## Value-chain stages
| Stage | Economic decision/control | Potential revenue or benefit | Costs, risks and evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Origination | Donor, adviser, wealth manager, employer | Adviser retention, referral, planning | Acquisition expense; conflicts and disclosure |
| Contribution and acceptance | Legal charitable sponsor | Administrative fee base; donor acquisition | Valuation, due diligence, tax substantiation, complex asset risk |
| Custody and investment | Custodian, adviser, asset manager, sponsor investment committee | AUM-based investment/advisory/custody fees, spread where applicable | Market, concentration, liquidity, fiduciary, investment selection |
| Fund accounting and servicing | Sponsor, administrator, technology supplier | Contract fees, subscription or per-account revenue | Ledger accuracy, reconciliations, security, service burden |
| Grant recommendation and diligence | Donor adviser, sponsor | Service differentiation; possibly sponsor-level fees | Eligibility, sanctions/fraud, grant controls, timing |
| Grant payment | Sponsor, bank, payment network, recipient | Payments economics where applicable | Settlement, errors, reconciliation, nonprofit receipt |
| Recipient relationship | Operating charity, advisers, intermediaries | Potential fundraising/treasury/endowment relationship | Consent, conflicts, independence and suitability |

**Critical distinction:** DAF assets legally belong to the sponsoring charity. The donor generally retains advisory privileges, not ownership or enforceable control over distributions. Commercial investment firms may manage assets but are not necessarily sponsors.

## Economic identities and double-count controls
- Beginning DAF assets + contributions + investment gains/losses - grants - sponsor fees - other outflows = ending DAF assets, with adjustments for transfers, timing and reporting definitions.
- **Sponsor Form 990 revenue is not equal to DAF administration revenue.** Contribution revenue, investment returns and accounting classifications can dominate.
- **Sponsor assets are not manager AUM by default.** Verify custody, discretionary authority, fee basis and whether assets are managed externally.
- **Gross fee pool is not additive profit.** Sponsor fees can pay an administrator; adviser fees may be bundled into an investment charge; payment charges may be pass-through.
- Model net benefit per participant only after intercompany eliminations, expense allocations, donor acquisition costs and risk costs.

## Required standardized institution template
For each sponsor record:
1. Legal entity/EIN, sponsor type, parent/affiliates, product segmentation.
2. Form 990 Schedule D DAF count, contributions, grants, end assets, financial-year basis; reconciliation to annual report.
3. Published sponsor fee schedule and effective realized fee if independently derivable.
4. Investment options, default pools, external advisers, custody and investment fees.
5. Donor origin channels and any employer/adviser/private-label relationships.
6. Administrator, platform, payments, fund accounting and outsourced functions with source confidence.
7. Recipient/grant restrictions, payout rules, donor privileges and compliance responsibilities.
8. Quantified or unquantified indirect value to parent or partners, with conflicts and alternatives.
9. Clarity assumption ledger and EDF control-point diagnosis.
10. Evidence date, primary source, denominator, uncertainty, competing hypothesis.

## First balanced comparison cohort
- Commercial: Fidelity Charitable, DAFgiving360, Vanguard Charitable.
- Independent: National Philanthropic Trust, American Endowment Foundation.
- Community: Silicon Valley Community Foundation, Chicago Community Trust.
- Faith/specialty: National Christian Foundation, Jewish Communal Fund.
- Bank/wealth-linked: Morgan Stanley GIFT, Goldman Sachs wealth-management DAF, Bank of America Charitable Gift Fund.
- Infrastructure/technology: Ren, TIFIN Give, Foundation Source, Chariot, Charityvest and Daffy as distinct roles, **not presumed direct competitors**.

## Hypotheses to test, not conclusions
H1: Investment management and donor relationship economics can exceed direct DAF platform fees for some integrated financial groups.
H2: Sponsor size reduces average operating costs, but complexity and service mix may offset scale economies.
H3: Adviser distribution and trust may matter more than donor-facing UI in some segments.
H4: The best operating model may remove unnecessary steps rather than automate legacy grant vetting.
H5: Faster grants and larger grant volumes are not equivalent to greater charitable impact without outcome and counterfactual evidence.
H6: Commercial sponsor revenue growth can diverge from grants to operating charities.

## Clarity evidence rules
Distinguish observed filings, disclosed commercial terms, credible inference, illustrative model and speculation. Every dollar claim needs entity, fiscal period, gross/net treatment, fee basis, source and reconciliation. Do not use unsupported supplier/customer attribution or user confidential experience as public proof.

## EDF diagnostic: failure and opportunity surfaces
Map decision rights, handoffs, duplicated data, reconciliation, manual exceptions, regulatory constraints, dependency concentration, integration reversibility and recipient outcomes. Identify the **actual bottleneck** and whether eliminating, standardizing or automating it improves measured outcomes.
