# DAF market demand, user behavior, operations and the data that would actually settle investment decisions

**Evidence assessment November 11, 2026.** This analysis extends the current 25-entity `daf-landscape` market map. It favors independent empirical research, distinguishes charitable outcomes from company economics and creates a ranked **27-question evidence backlog**. No new vendor tests, investor-only records, customer records, confidential adviser data or tax-professional sign-offs were obtained.

## Executive synthesis

**The next useful DAF research is not another vendor brochure.** The 2026-revised national statistics reveal a much larger and more heterogeneous ecosystem than a typical advisor-led DAF product comparison suggests; peer-reviewed/independent research shows donor and sponsor behavioral differences; recipient-side transaction research reveals an alternative opportunity in donor attribution and stewardship. Together, these require **three different economic and performance models**:

1. **Sponsor and enterprise technology:** 501(c)(3) operators buy donor/fund accounting, grant administration, custody reconciliation, compliance and white-label systems. The measurable business unit is an *actual paying institution and contracted service*, not a count of donor accounts.
2. **Advisor and donor decision support:** Wealth firms may distribute DAFs, but donors often give through multiple channels and require direct-vs-DAF/no-DAF comparisons. The correct metric is *advisor/donor suitability, qualified conversion, net cost, and retained relationship*, not merely "DAF accounts opened."
3. **Nonprofit recipient and payment infrastructure:** Frequent smaller DAF grants need accurate attribution, approved execution, settlement and acknowledgement. Transaction throughput, match quality and payment reliability matter more here than invested DAF balances. This is a distinct Chariot-like infrastructure market.

**No company currently wins across these markets based on executed evidence**; vendor-marketed capabilities do not settle cost, software gross margins, funded-account conversion, safety or vendor retention.

## 1. The market baseline has been revised. Do not carry forward stale totals.

The Donor Advised Fund Research Collaborative (DAFRC) **Spring 2026 revised FY2024 national analysis** supersedes its initially published 2025 figures [B01,B02]:

| U.S. donor-advised fund measure, **FY2024** | Revised observation |
|---|---:|
| Sponsoring organizations | **1,512** |
| National sponsors | 103 |
| Community foundation sponsors | 803 |
| Single-issue sponsors | 606 |
| DAF accounts, rounded | **3.59 million** |
| Sponsor-held charitable assets | **$327.87 billion** |
| Contributions to DAFs | **$90.57 billion** |
| Grants out of DAFs | **$64.60 billion** |
| Aggregate payout rate | **25.2%** |

These are **FY2024** figures compiled from IRS filings, not realtime 2026 customer data. The source explicitly attributes a large portion of reported account growth to **donation-processing accounts**. Hence (3.59\text{ million accounts}\) cannot be assumed to represent 3.59 million long-term, invested, advisor-advised DAF relationships or buyers of Ren/Foundations Source enterprise software.

**Reconciliation caution:** The Urban Institute's separate dashboard still surfaced **$89.6 billion of FY2024 DAF contributions**, while the Spring 2026 revised DAFRC release reports **$90.57 billion** [B01,B15]. This may reflect a different snapshot or inclusion/exclusion method. We have **not downloaded and compared their sponsor-year raw datasets**; preserve both source dates rather than silently averaging or overwriting. That reconciliation is backlog Q25.

### What is the actual addressable market?

**Do not simply multiply total assets by an advertised platform basis-point rate.** Some assets are held by charitable sponsors using in-house operations, some are touched by technology without being billed asset-wise, some have service contracts under fixed/usage fees, and donation processors have unusually different unit economics.

Correct market model: **legal sponsor cohort → software and operating model → paying enterprise contract → active funded DAF accounts → true billable asset/transactions → recognized vendor revenue → cost to serve.**

Three missing inputs dominate valuation: (a) how many sponsors/institutions actually buy software, (b) contracted fixed vs asset- and transaction-linked fee bases, (c) gross cost and retention at the paying-entity level. All remain unverified across vendors [Q01–Q05].

## 2. Account-level longitudinal evidence makes the case for a behavioral model

The independent **2024 National Study** covers nine years, **111 voluntarily participating programs**, more than 50,000 DAF accounts, over 600,000 contributions and more than 2.25 million grants [B03,B16]. In the three-year period ending **2022**:
- **78%** of accounts made at least one grant; approximately **22% made none**.
- The **median account-level three-year averaged payout rate was 9%** (15% for active accounts), distinct from a national aggregate asset-weighted payout calculation.
- **49%** of sampled funds held less than **$50,000** at 2021 year-end; 7% held $1 million or more.
- **57% of contributions** were made in Q4, versus **32% of grants**. This creates an asymmetric year-end asset-contribution workload but a relatively year-round grant servicing need.
- **54%** granted at least half the original contribution within three years; grant-activity and principal-spend rates capture different behaviors.

These are historical, sponsor-voluntary *account-level* figures, **not national FY2024 account inactivity**, platform churn, or evidence that tax incentives caused account opening. They support a segmented operational and product hypothesis:

**A product designed for occasional or dormant donors should not be evaluated by the same metrics as high-touch ultra-high-net-worth complex-asset accounts.** Test whether reactivation, grant reminders, beneficiary/successor updates and seamless donor authorization improve real charitable outcomes. Do not claim a causal uplift until a matched-control experiment exists.

The NPT **FY2025** annual report illustrates skew within one sponsor: **median balance ~$79,700 versus mean ~$2.7 million** among about 20,561 accounts [B10]. A customer value model based on a mean account rather than a distribution will overstate ordinary account economics.

## 3. Surveyed donors use multiple giving channels and not always outside investment managers

The independent **2025 DAFRC survey of more than 2,100 donors** [B04] reports:
- Only **9%** said an outside financial adviser, rather than the DAF sponsor, managed the DAF's **investments**. This is **not** the percent who receive charitable planning advice or use a wealth adviser for other accounts.
- **48%** said more than 75% of their charitable contributions over the past three years passed through DAFs. That means a substantial other group also gifts directly or uses other vehicles.
- Among respondents reporting lapses, **53%** cited giving from other sources as a reason; a lapse from DAF granting does not necessarily mean declining total generosity.
- Respondent average age was **67**, with **96% reporting net worth above $1 million**. This sample should **not** be generalized to micro-funded donation processor accounts.
- **72% of survey respondents who gave anonymously** said avoiding public recognition was a motivation. A recipient's lack of donor identity and a donor's deliberate preference for confidentiality must not be conflated.

This evidence strengthens our previous **direct giving / DAF / cash / invested / no-DAF** suitability cases. An effective technology vendor should sometimes *not open a DAF* and should preserve the donor's intentionally selected privacy. Optimizing "DAF starts" alone can be misaligned with tax and charitable outcomes.

## 4. Sponsor operators report a potentially larger problem outside grant vetting

DAFRC's independent **manager survey of 128 sponsors** (2023 survey, published 2025) [B05] reports:

| Sponsor manager self-rating | Percentage |
|---|---:|
| Adequate information technology | **61%** |
| Adequate staffing | **52%** |
| Very/extremely effective at grant review | **86%** |
| Very/extremely effective at philanthropic advising | **55%** |
| Very/extremely effective at soliciting DAF contributions | **34%** |
| Full donor name and contact information delivered **by default** with grants | **36%** |

These are *manager perceptions*, not measured software error rates or causal intervention results. But they challenge the assumption that expensive AI grant vetting should be the principal differentiation. Grant review is relatively well-rated by respondents; donor acquisition/engagement and staffing capacity are weaker. An opportunity may exist in **reducing manual exceptions, improving donor advice and completing grant acknowledgements correctly with lawful donor identity**.

**Privacy counterweight:** Only 36% sharing full contact info by default is not itself a defect. Many donors genuinely prefer anonymity. The missing research is **consent-aware, accurate recipient attribution**: when donors *allow* identifying details, what percentage of approved grants arrives with enough data to recognize, thank and retain the supporter?

## 5. Recipients show a high-volume, midlevel giving use case; do not confuse retention

The **May 2026 K2D Strategies / Chariot nonprofit study** [B08], summarized by Candid [B09], observed **54 participating nonprofits**, **$26.1 billion total giving**, **$3.3 billion DAF giving**, and about **907,000 DAF gift transactions**. Its researchers reported:
- Roughly **two-thirds of observed DAF gifts were under $1,000** (share of *gift count*, not asset balances).
- DAF donors had **13 percentage-point higher nonprofit donor-retention** than non-DAF donors in the participant dataset.
- Participating nonprofits' median DAF giving revenue grew **75% from 2021 to 2025**, compared with reported **9%** median non-DAF revenue growth.
- Participants who shifted giving methods from other channels to a DAF can differ in wealth, causes and intent; the study does **not demonstrate** that DAF products caused increased generosity.

**Evidence class:** A large *recipient-side observational* dataset, produced in partnership with **Chariot**, which has a commercial interest in encouraging DAF payments. Useful operational data, **not independent randomized proof of Chariot's product impact** and not necessarily a representative national nonprofit cohort.

**Most important distinction:** "13 percentage points higher nonprofit donor retention" is **not** 13 percentage points better Ren/TIFIN/Chariot software customer retention, not a grantmaker contract renewal metric, and not proof that a DAF caused the gap. Do not put this into a software company's NRR model.

## 6. Financial adviser demand is real, but it is a funnel

DAFRC's 2026 national study surveyed **669 financial advisors** and ran six focus groups [B06]. A public summary reports **75%** engaged clients in moderate/substantial philanthropic discussions [B07]. We have not examined the full underlying advisor executive PDF because the research portal's PDF link was not retrievable.

**Missing conversion measurements:** advisor charitable conversation → verified intent → direct/DAF alternatives → recommended vehicle → actual accepted donated asset → funded DAF → donor grant engagement → retained adviser relationship. Each requires a different denominator and date. Partner announcements and firm-wide adviser AUM **do not provide these stage-level conversions**. A larger client wealth tier does not automatically mean greater need for a DAF.

The DAFRC sponsor manager study also says the **median sponsor estimates only half of donors use offered philanthropic advisory services** [B05]. This is a product adoption question, not proof such advice is intrinsically valuable. We should measure qualified donor outcomes and support hours, not merely whether advice was offered.

## 7. Noncash assets, seasonality, and donor identity shape actual software work

Fidelity Charitable reports **69% of CY2025 contributions were noncash** and **77% of grants went to nonprofits previously supported** [B11]. These are Fidelity-specific sponsor figures; they cannot be applied indiscriminately to every charity or software vendor.

Combined with DAFRC's older seasonality evidence, these suggest **three operational datasets**:
- Gift-acceptance chain: security type, marketability, liquidity/restriction, source tax lot, donor/sponsor authorization, effective gift date, custody acceptance, valuation, liquidation, receipt issuance, failure reasons.
- Grant lifecycle: source DAF account, recommendation, sponsor approval, eligibility, payment authorization, check/ACH event, nonprofit actual receipt, rejection/return, final reconciliation and grant acknowledgement.
- Donor engagement: fund status, activity history, privacy choice, successor plans, giving across other channels (where consented), approved contact sharing, return gifting and reactivation.

**Without these event records, claims such as "instant grants," "automated tax optimization," "better donor retention," or "90% operational savings" cannot be fairly adjudicated.**

## 8. Regulatory monitoring is a separate, versioned evidence stream

The **September 29, 2026 Treasury/IRS 2026–2027 Priority Guidance Plan** specifically lists **future guidance under sections 4966 and 6033 relating to certain DAF arrangements** [B12]. This is **planned guidance**, not a final rule, new deadline, or statement of regulatory noncompliance.

The underlying law already governs certain DAF taxable distributions and sponsor/manager liability [B13,B14]. Regulatory tests should separately capture:
- Actual effective law, regulation and scope of donor/sponsor program.
- Date and version of applicable IRS guidance; future plans cannot be coded as current law.
- Donor/private benefit and prohibited distributions, grant approval roles, expenditure responsibility exceptions, information reporting, and transaction audit lineage.
- Test evidence with qualified legal/tax review. No public source here establishes a DAF vendor violation.

**Decision value:** A vendor with robust versioned compliance controls may be more durable than one that offers only persuasive descriptions of AI review.

## 9. Ranked research backlog

[Machine-readable ranked 27-gap backlog](../data/decision-critical-data-backlog-2026.csv) provides for every entry: priority, unit, precise unanswered question, measurement, data route, currently known and unknown, source IDs, access class, business decision and evidence acceptance criterion.

### First nine questions, which could change the business/investment conclusion

| P0 | High-leverage measurement | Why it changes the decision |
|---|---|---|
| **Q01** | Split DAFRC sponsor accounts into *traditional invested* versus donation processing | Correct serviceable market / account denominators |
| **Q02** | Count **actual paying external platform customers** among sponsors and wealth institutions | Vendor TAM and company share, not charitable accounts |
| **Q03** | Interview/observe sponsor procurement and real pain ranking | Whether provider differentiation addresses actual buying reasons |
| **Q04** | Fully-loaded **cost per successfully settled grant** and per fund | Integrated operations versus software margin claims |
| **Q05** | Customer-level fixed/asset-linked/usage revenue cohorts and GRR/NRR | Customer durability and enterprise valuation |
| **Q06** | P50/P95 stages from grant recommendation to **external recipient receipt** and unreconciled failures | Chariot vs operator-native rails and sponsor controls |
| **Q07** | **Consent-adjusted nonprofit donor match** rate and acknowledgement time | Recipient infrastructure and donor relationship value |
| **Q08** | Independent CPA-reviewed **direct / DAF / no-DAF** donor tax fit outputs, then vendor execution | Recommendation quality rather than marketing features |
| **Q09** | Account distribution by size, funding type, activity and age at each provider | Correct customer unit economics and package/product choice |

**P1 (ten questions):** reactivation causal impact, advisor-to-funded conversion, noncash gift workflow, seasonal capacity, donor privacy, regulatory versioning, grant exceptions, nonlinear nonprofit donor impact, vendor fee capture and migration continuity.

**P2 (eight questions):** cash versus invested take-up, philanthropic advising uptake, scoped SOC audit assurance, processor gross-to-net, sponsor type variation, release/version reconciliation, governed AI value tests, recipient attribution improvements.

These are research priorities, **not existing verified vendor deficiencies**. A source register or blog story can define the question but does not settle it.

## 10. Suggested next collection sequence

**Public-data stage (can proceed without vendor access):** download *actual* revised DAFRC FY2024 sponsor-level dataset and technical appendix; reconcile sponsor IDs/categories, donation processors and versioned years; compare audited Form 990/Schedule D sponsor metrics without treating expense lines as supplier income; enrich grant recipient and consent literature. The prior live public site indexed downloadable data, but its external PDF redirect blocked direct fetching. **No raw file was ingested in this project.**

**Authorized stage (requires sponsors/vendors):** recruit representative sponsor/wealth/nonprofit cohorts, observe anonymized operational event logs and invoices, and conduct limited synthetic DAF lifecycle and tax-choice tests. Obtain written rights for benchmarks and confidential information before collecting.

**Analytical stage:** run matched-cohort and controlled interventions, record all negative outcomes and denominator definitions, audit data lineage. Use multi-year sponsor customer and nonprofit donor retention as **different metrics**. Financial conclusions should require both revenue and cost, and vendor claims need either a witnessed demo or independent source.

The [source register](../data/market-demand-evidence-sources-2026.csv) contains **18 qualified public sources**; the [observed metric register](../data/market-demand-observed-metrics-2026.csv) contains **33 dated, population-specific statistics**; the [research protocol](evidence-collection-design-2026.md) defines collection events and evaluation gates. **None represents newly tested vendor performance.**

### Decision

The stronger software thesis is **complete charitable decision-to-outcome integrity**, not just grant vetting or quicker payouts: match a legitimate donor decision to the correct charitable vehicle, accepted asset, legal sponsor, transparent costs, approved grant, actual nonprofit receipt and a usable donor acknowledgement. The platform that performs that lifecycle correctly and cheaply at scale might have a defensible advantage.

That remains a **hypothesis**. We need observable matched costs, donor outcomes and retention data to know whether any current vendor has achieved it.
